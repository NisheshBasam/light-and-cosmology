The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum is the full range of electromagnetic waves, from low-energy radio waves on one end all the way up to high-energy gamma rays on the other [web:8][web:41].

Simple electromagnetic spectrum diagram from radio (low energy) to gamma rays (high energy) [web:30][web:33].

From lowest energy / longest wavelength to highest energy / shortest wavelength, the spectrum goes: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays [web:30][web:33].

Radio Waves

Longest wavelength, lowest frequency and energy [web:30].

Uses: radio broadcasting, TV, communication [web:41].

Microwaves

Shorter wavelength than radio, higher frequency [web:30].

Uses: microwave ovens, radar, satellite links [web:41].

Infrared

Felt as heat, just beyond red visible light [web:41].

Uses: remote controls, thermal imaging [web:41].

Visible Light

The only part of the spectrum human eyes can see [web:30].

Uses: vision, photography, fiber optics [web:24].

Ultraviolet

Higher energy than visible; can cause sunburn [web:30].

Uses: sterilization, black lights [web:41].

X-rays

Very high energy, can pass through soft tissue [web:30].

Uses: medical imaging, security scanners [web:41].

Gamma Rays

Highest energy, shortest wavelength [web:30].

Uses: cancer treatment, studying violent space events [web:41].

Key Equations & Relationships

Frequency and Wavelength

In a vacuum, the speed of light is \( c \approx 3.0 \times 10^{8} \) m/s, and it relates wavelength \( \lambda \) and frequency \( f \) through \( c = \lambda f \) [web:30].

As frequency increases, wavelength decreases, so high-frequency waves like X-rays and gamma rays have very short wavelengths [web:30][web:33].

Photon Energy

The energy of a single photon is given by \( E = h f \), where \( h \approx 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \) J·s is Planck’s constant [web:33].

This means higher-frequency light has higher-energy photons, which is why ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are more dangerous to living tissue [web:33].

Ionizing vs Non-ionizing Radiation

Non-ionizing radiation (radio, microwaves, infrared, visible) does not normally have enough energy to knock electrons off atoms [web:33].

Ionizing radiation (ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays) has photons energetic enough to ionize atoms and molecules, which can damage cells [web:33].

Light as a Wave and a Particle

Light behaves like a wave when it shows interference and diffraction, such as in the double-slit experiment where overlapping waves create bright and dark fringes [web:30].

Light also behaves like a particle, because it comes in discrete packets called photons that transfer energy in chunks, as seen in the photoelectric effect [web:33].

Wave–particle duality means that light is described as an electromagnetic wave and as a stream of photons, depending on the experiment and how it is observed [web:30][web:33].

Life Cycle of Stars

Simplified life cycle of a star: low-mass path (white dwarf) and high-mass path (supernova, neutron star, black hole) [web:28][web:37].

How Stars Form

Stars form inside large clouds of gas and dust called nebulae; gravity pulls this material together into a protostar, which heats up until nuclear fusion can begin in the core [web:28][web:37].

Once hydrogen fusion starts, the object becomes a main-sequence star and stays in that stage for most of its lifetime [web:28][web:37].

Low-mass Stars (like the Sun)

Low-mass stars form from nebulae, contract into protostars, and then spend billions of years as main-sequence stars, steadily fusing hydrogen into helium [web:28][web:37].

When core hydrogen runs out, they expand into red giants, then shed their outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving behind a white dwarf that slowly cools [web:37][web:34].

High-mass Stars

High-mass stars also form in nebulae and pass through the protostar and main-sequence stages, but they burn their fuel much faster because their cores are hotter [web:37][web:28].

After becoming supergiants and fusing heavier elements in layers, they end their lives in powerful supernova explosions, leaving behind neutron stars or black holes [web:37][web:34].

Who Burns Out Faster?

High-mass stars burn out way faster, sometimes only lasting a few million years, while smaller stars like the Sun can keep shining for billions of years [web:28][web:34]. In simple terms, the bigger and hotter the star, the shorter its life.

How Heavier Elements Form

In stars, fusion first turns hydrogen into helium and later builds elements like carbon and oxygen; in massive stars and supernovae, even heavier elements are created and spread into space [web:28][web:37].

These heavy elements eventually become part of new stars, planets, and living things, meaning we are literally made of recycled star material [web:28][web:37].

Hertzsprung–Russell (H-R) Diagram

Simplified H-R diagram: main sequence from hot, bright blue stars to cool, dim red stars, plus giants and white dwarfs [web:31][web:34].

An H-R diagram plots a star’s luminosity on the vertical axis and its surface temperature or spectral type on the horizontal axis, with temperature decreasing from left to right [web:31][web:49].

Most stars fall along the main sequence, a diagonal band from hot, bright blue stars to cool, dim red stars, while giants and supergiants lie above and white dwarfs lie below [web:31][web:34].

At the same temperature, a bigger star has a higher luminosity, so large stars appear higher on the diagram; hotter stars appear bluer, and cooler stars appear redder [web:31][web:49].

The Sun and Star Spectra

The Sun is mostly hydrogen and helium, with small amounts of heavier elements; we know this by studying its spectrum and matching absorption lines to elements measured in the lab [web:34][web:31].

Each chemical element produces a unique set of spectral lines, like a barcode; by comparing a star’s spectrum to known patterns, astronomers can identify which elements are present [web:32][web:34].

This technique, called spectroscopy, lets us learn the composition, temperature, and motion of stars, even though we cannot visit them directly [web:32][web:28].

Redshift & Blueshift

Redshift moves spectral lines toward longer wavelengths (right); blueshift moves them toward shorter wavelengths (left) [web:29].

What is Redshift?

Redshift happens when light from an object gets stretched out to longer wavelengths, so its spectral lines slide toward the red side of the spectrum [web:29].

That tells us the object is moving away from us, or that space between us and the object is expanding [web:29].

What is Blueshift?

Blueshift is the opposite: light is compressed to shorter wavelengths, so spectral lines move toward the blue side of the spectrum [web:29].

This indicates that the object is moving toward us along our line of sight [web:29].

How to Tell if a Star is Moving Toward or Away

To determine a star’s motion, astronomers compare its spectral lines to a reference spectrum from the same element measured in a lab [web:32][web:29].

If the star’s lines are shifted toward longer wavelengths, the star is redshifted and moving away; if the lines are shifted toward shorter wavelengths, the star is blueshifted and moving toward us [web:32][web:29].
